From hospital walls to community life: WHO sets out a difficult transition

WHO’s guide describes the service, housing and rights safeguards needed for long-stay psychiatric patients to live in communities.

Health & Humanity · Explainer · Original source: World Health Organization, 9 October 2026

What must exist outside a hospital before someone can safely leave it? A home is one part of the answer. So are continuing health and social supports, the ability to make decisions, suitable housing, a way to reconnect with people, and access to work or daily activities when wanted. Without them, the language of freedom can conceal a transfer of burden rather than genuine choice.

On 9 October 2026, the World Health Organization published a practical guide to moving from long-stay psychiatric institutional care to community-based mental health and social care. The guide focuses on adults who have experienced lengthy stays in psychiatric hospitals. It is intended for hospital leaders and decision-makers, in partnership with people using services, their families, communities and public authorities.

The document is not a call to end every form of hospital care immediately. Nor does it claim that sending someone home without follow-up is an adequate substitute for care. Its subject is the planned transformation of a system that can isolate people for long periods, restrict their choices and struggle to provide meaningful life beyond hospital walls.

What does deinstitutionalization mean here?

Deinstitutionalization is often shortened to DI. In this context it means enabling people who have spent long periods—WHO's press release describes six months or longer—in psychiatric hospitals to live in communities with the care, opportunities and safeguards they need. The process requires more than a change of address. It involves rethinking clinical services, housing, social support, human rights protections and how responsibility passes between institutions.

The WHO guide identifies a system-wide challenge: institutions may consume substantial resources while separating people from ordinary social life. Yet community-based services cannot be assumed to exist merely because a policy announces a transition. Planning has to ask what happens on an ordinary Tuesday, long after a formal discharge has been recorded.

Three connected phases: plan, prepare, provide

Plan: Understand who is living in long-stay care, listen to their preferences, assess needs and rights, map available supports, and agree on responsibilities and resources. Planning must include the people affected, not only administrative offices.

Prepare: Build or strengthen the services that will make community living feasible. This can include supported housing, coordination across health and social systems, workforce support, transitions and safeguards. Preparing a destination requires more than reserving a bed elsewhere.

Provide: Deliver and sustain the support in real settings, monitor continuity, address emerging problems and adapt to individual choices. The guide organizes actions around reducing admissions and lengths of stay, improving care within hospitals, and expanding supported discharge and participation outside them.

These phases are not a three-click checklist. They overlap in practice, and implementation depends on laws, funding, workers, local services, people with lived experience and their communities. The guidance recognizes leadership, partnerships and responsibilities across institutions and authorities.

What is the difference between independence and abandonment?

Independence does not always mean doing everything alone. For one person, autonomy may involve deciding where to live while receiving help with daily routines. For another, it may mean access to a familiar team, supported employment, or a chance to rejoin community activities. Supports should increase a person's agency, not erase it.

A system can make two opposite mistakes. It may hold people in restrictive settings because alternatives were never built. Or it may discharge people into unsuitable situations and call the result reform. A rights-based approach rejects both shortcuts. It asks what choices are possible, what continuity is in place, and how the person's preferences and safety will be respected.

It is equally important to avoid a false picture of people living with mental health conditions as a uniform group. People's experiences, needs, circumstances and desired levels of support differ. The guide cannot replace individualized planning or legal safeguards in each jurisdiction.

Why publish the guide now?

WHO's October announcement, issued ahead of World Mental Health Day, argues that long-stay institutional models continue to limit autonomy and expose some people to rights violations. The new 146-page practical guide is a resource for changing how services operate. It builds on earlier WHO rights-based community mental health guidance, but its focus is operational: what leaders and partners must do to enable transitions.

WHO reported that nearly half of people in the settings it described were held involuntarily. That figure should be interpreted in the scope of WHO's release, not generalized to every mental health facility worldwide or presented as a newly measured global prevalence without fuller methodological information.

How could progress be evaluated?

Counting hospital discharges alone would produce an incomplete picture. Useful questions include whether people have suitable housing, whether services remain available after transition, whether they have meaningful choice, whether emergency support works, and whether they feel connected to places and activities that matter. Some measures are numerical; others require listening carefully to lived experience.

Suppose two programmes report the same reduction in long-stay admissions. In one, community supports are stable, people are heard and re-admissions decrease. In the other, people encounter housing instability and gaps in care. The numbers could look similar while the lived outcomes are profoundly different. This is a hypothetical comparison, not a claim about any country or programme.

What remains open?

The WHO guide offers a framework, not proof that a country has implemented it successfully. Local laws, financing, workforce capacity, housing supply and protections differ. Implementation studies and accounts from people directly affected will be necessary to understand whether promised transitions improve real life.

For readers in Lebanon or elsewhere, this is international guidance—not a description of a national service that is already functioning. The transferable lesson is a question of system design: If a person steps through a hospital's door, is there a genuinely supported life on the other side?
