Scientists mapped the brain’s action network cell by cell

An NIH-supported atlas explores the cells and development of the basal ganglia, with limits for interpreting disease and treatment.

The basal ganglia help connect learning, movement and the selection of actions. A landmark research collection now describes the cells in these structures at extraordinary resolution — but a cellular atlas is not a map of a person's intentions.

You reach for a cup, change your mind and put your hand back on the table. The movement is ordinary enough to disappear into the day. Yet the brain processes that make it possible remain remarkably difficult to study. Scientists can identify large structures, observe patterns of activity and measure behavior. Connecting those large-scale observations to the many kinds of individual cells involved is a different challenge.

On 12 November 2026, the US National Institutes of Health announced a research collection from its BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network (BICAN) focused on the basal ganglia: interconnected brain structures strongly implicated in movement control, learning and action selection. The initiative brings together maps of molecular identity, anatomical location and development, and makes tools available for researchers to explore and compare the findings. The NIH announcement describes a collection of 27 papers, rather than a single experiment that explains behavior. [1]

A map of what, exactly?

An ordinary geographical atlas tells us where places sit relative to one another. A cell atlas has to answer more questions: which cell populations can be distinguished, what molecular markers help define them, where they are located, and how those descriptions compare across specimens, developmental periods or species.

That is important because two cells can look similar under a microscope yet differ in the genes they express or the circuits in which they participate. Conversely, scientists working in different laboratories may assign different names to related cellular populations. A shared reference can make their findings more comparable.

The BICAN programme integrates multiple kinds of measurements. Some techniques inspect RNA, a readout of which genes a cell is using at the time of sampling. Others examine regulatory features or add location information by studying tissue sections. Computational methods bring these descriptions into common reference coordinates. The resulting atlas is a research model built from multiple samples and measurements, not an image taken from a living person's thoughts. [1, 2]

The scientific journey in three stages

1. Identify the cells. Research teams work with donated human brain specimens and appropriately governed biological material from model species. Single-cell or single-nucleus sequencing helps distinguish molecular profiles. Sequencing here means reading molecular signals associated with cells; it does not mean watching a person make a decision.

2. Put identities in space. Spatial imaging and reference anatomy help locate different populations in the basal ganglia. A name without a location is limited: researchers want to know whether a type is concentrated in a particular region, varies through development, or appears in comparable places across species.

3. Compare evidence, then formulate new questions. With a common reference, researchers can revisit data related to conditions such as Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease or autism. They can ask whether particular cell populations show associated changes. Some follow-on experiments test targeted methods in mice. Those are research directions, not clinical results for patients. [1]

Why do the basal ganglia matter?

The basal ganglia are not one compact 'decision button'. They form interacting loops with other parts of the brain. One component, the striatum, receives a great deal of incoming information. Basal-ganglia circuits contribute to how actions are selected, learned and adjusted, alongside the cortex, thalamus, brainstem and the rest of the nervous system.

This matters for everyday life because an action is rarely just a muscle contraction. There is a goal, a setting, a history of previous experience, available information and often a need to inhibit an alternative. But it would be a mistake to point to one newly described cell group and claim that it explains why somebody made a particular choice. The new reference sharpens questions about circuitry; it does not replace psychological, social or behavioral explanations.

The promising results — and the distance still to travel

NIH highlighted studies using the atlas to look for cell-population differences associated with autism-related datasets and to guide experimental targeting in mouse models relevant to Parkinson's disease. That is notable precisely because more detailed classification can reveal patterns that broad averages may conceal. [1]

Yet a pattern associated with a condition is not automatically its cause, and a result in a mouse does not establish safety or benefit in a person. Human basal-ganglia samples generally involve postmortem donations; these provide valuable molecular detail but cannot directly measure the intentions or lived experience of the donors at the time of a behavior. Researchers must also consider differences among donors, technical methods, ages and species.

One accessible BICAN reference paper, A Community Standard Multispecies Cell Atlas of the Basal Ganglia, was posted as a bioRxiv preprint in April 2026. It describes harmonized reference systems and cross-species comparison. Because that particular version was not journal peer reviewed at the time recorded by PubMed Central, it should be labeled as a preprint rather than quietly treated as a peer-reviewed clinical finding. [2]

What becomes possible with a shared reference?

The value of an atlas may lie as much in its infrastructure as in any individual result. A researcher in one laboratory can compare molecular classifications with a dataset generated elsewhere. A scientist studying development can relate a cell population to an adult reference. A team working on disease mechanisms can test whether its signal concerns a broad region or a more specific population.

Think of a city that once had only a road map and now has an organized, searchable directory of the buildings along those roads. That directory does not explain why a resident went to a particular building yesterday. But it enables more precise questions about what the city contains, how its parts connect, and where unexpected problems appear.

The analogy has limits — cells are dynamic, not buildings — but it captures the central achievement: a more consistent language for scientific investigation.

The three questions to carry forward

  1. Which cell-type observations remain stable when additional human samples are included?
  2. Which molecular differences can be linked to circuit function, rather than merely correlated with a diagnosis or condition?
  3. How can future research connect cellular references with behavior without reducing human agency to a diagram?

The development is newsworthy today because it changes the tools researchers can use. Its longer-term importance will depend on what those tools help others discover — and on how carefully scientists distinguish cell maps, explanations of behavior and evidence for treatment.

Essential terms

  • Basal ganglia: a set of interconnected deep brain structures important for movement, learning and action selection, among other functions.
  • Striatum: a major input region within basal-ganglia circuitry; it includes the caudate nucleus and putamen.
  • Cell type: a group of cells described through a combination of molecular, anatomical and sometimes physiological properties.
  • Transcriptomics: study of RNA produced by cells, used to investigate patterns of gene activity.
  • Spatial mapping: placing measurements in anatomical context rather than treating them as an unordered list.
  • Preprint: a publicly shared scholarly manuscript that has not necessarily undergone journal peer review.
