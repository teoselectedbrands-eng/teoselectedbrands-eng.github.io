Can a wristband tell us how a country moves?

A draft WHO device profile asks how wearables can measure activity fairly and consistently across populations.

Technology & AI · Explainer · Original source: World Health Organization, 8 November 2026

A device can count movement. But can it count the ways a population lives? That distinction sits at the centre of a World Health Organization consultation launched on 8 November 2026. The organization is asking researchers, governments, manufacturers and members of the public what a useful wrist-worn wearable device should be capable of measuring if it is to help countries understand physical activity and sedentary behaviour.

This is not an announcement that WHO has certified a new smartwatch, chosen a manufacturer or published a binding universal technical standard. It is an invitation to shape a draft target product profile, often abbreviated TPP: a description of minimum and desirable features that a device should have for a particular public-health purpose. Feedback is open until 6 December 2026. The final specifications are not yet settled.

Why begin with the population, not the individual?

A consumer fitness tracker can be useful to someone interested in their personal routine. Public health asks a different kind of question: Are people across different ages, regions or circumstances finding meaningful opportunities to move? Are patterns changing over time? Are comparisons between two surveys trustworthy? These questions concern groups, not a diagnosis or an individual judgment of effort.

National monitoring has often used questionnaires, asking participants to remember how often or how long they moved. Surveys are relatively practical to distribute across diverse settings, but their answers depend on memory, wording and interpretation. Wearable sensors offer a possible complementary measurement of movement over time. They do not automatically remove every bias or explain why someone moves more or less.

The WHO proposal is explicitly directed at population health monitoring systems. The device is a potential measurement instrument for researchers and public agencies, not a prescription for everyone to buy a watch.

What does a wrist-worn device actually observe?

Imagine three layers. The first is a signal: a sensor detects physical changes such as acceleration at the wrist. The second is an interpretation: software estimates what those signals may mean for activity or sedentary behaviour. The third is a public-health estimate: analysts combine many people's measurements, document their methods and produce a result for a defined population.

Each layer adds assumptions. A wrist may move while a person is seated, or remain relatively still during an activity that has physical demands elsewhere in the body. A device's placement, fit, battery life, wear time and the way data are processed can affect what is recorded. And a national estimate also depends on which participants received devices and how missing data were handled. These are measurement questions—not evidence that wearables are inherently useless.

For this reason, WHO is asking not only about device specifications but also data access, practical deployment, implementation costs and use across varied national circumstances. A technically sophisticated device may be unsuitable for a survey if it cannot be maintained or used consistently across communities with limited resources.

Four questions readers can ask of any measurement

What is being measured? Movement at the wrist is not identical to every possible definition of physical activity, health, participation or function. The measure needs an operational definition: a precise statement of what counts.

Who is represented? A sample is the selected group whose measurements stand in for a larger population. If some groups are missing or devices are difficult for them to use, the results may not describe the wider population accurately.

Can the measurement be compared? Results from two devices—or two software versions—are not automatically equivalent. Validity concerns how well a measure captures its intended concept; reliability concerns whether it produces sufficiently consistent results under comparable conditions. Neither quality is guaranteed by a polished dashboard.

Who controls the data? Physical activity information can be sensitive when combined with time, location or other records. Public-health use calls for proportionate collection, clear stewardship, meaningful safeguards and local governance. WHO's draft invites comments on access and implementation; this report does not claim that a final privacy framework has already been adopted.

The consultation is also a question of equity

WHO explicitly welcomes feedback from low- and middle-income countries, as well as people and organizations involved in statistics, public health, standards and technology. A common framework may make comparison easier—but only if practical requirements are realistic. Connectivity, costs, technical support, language and accessibility all matter. A device that works well in a controlled laboratory might not be the best tool for a large, repeated survey.

The invitation is open until 6 December 2026. WHO plans to use the responses to revise the draft and inform subsequent discussions and guidance. It would be inaccurate to present the draft as a finished purchasing recommendation or to promise that better sensors alone will make communities more active.

The wider story: evidence is never the whole experience

This consultation follows other WHO work documenting a worldwide gap in adolescent physical activity. The two developments connect methodologically: good decisions need good measurements. But neither a survey answer nor a sensor trace reveals every part of a person's life—whether the route was safe, the playground welcoming, the public space accessible or the day simply overwhelming.

A responsible system can hold both truths at once. Measurement helps identify patterns. Human observation and dialogue help interpret what those patterns mean. Better questions emerge when we resist mistaking one for the other.

What remains uncertain: the eventual WHO product profile, which technologies will meet it, adoption by any country and how accurately particular devices will work in varied populations. These are questions for later reporting—not achievements already announced.
