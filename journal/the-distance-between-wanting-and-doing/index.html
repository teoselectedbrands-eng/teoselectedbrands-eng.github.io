The Distance Between Wanting and Doing

An independent evidence-informed essay about intentions, action, cues and the environment.

Aetherion JournalPublished: 14 November 202611 min reading
This is a separate research-informed essay. Illustrative scenes are not reporting of specific individuals. Editorial standards.

Aetherion Journal · Research-informed essay · Psychology, design and everyday functioning
Publication: 14 November 2026 · Not a news report or a clinical guide

We often mistake a plan for a beginning. Between the moment a person wants to do something and the moment the action becomes possible lies an entire world of timing, setting, memory, opportunity and friction.

A cup is waiting in the sink. Someone has promised themselves they will wash it when they finish reading. The promise is clear; the task is small. Later, the cup is still there. What happened between an intention that seemed perfectly sincere and an action that never began?

This is an invented, ordinary scene. It is not a case study, and the cup has no diagnostic significance. But it makes visible a question whose simplicity is deceptive: why does wanting to act not guarantee that we act?

Our everyday language frequently leaps over the gap. We say someone should 'just start', as though beginning is a tiny, effortless step located immediately after deciding. We praise determination as though it were a motor capable of pulling an intention into the world on its own. Yet action is neither a thought with feet nor a command sent down a single wire. It is an encounter between a person and a situation.

Intention has a different shape from action

An intention concerns a possible future. I want to walk after dinner. I want to call a friend. I want to return to an unfinished project. The intention may have emotional force, but it has not yet survived a real evening with its interruptions, weather, fatigue, competing demands, social obligations and missing equipment.

Action belongs to time and place. A walk needs a moment to leave, a route, sufficient safety and a reason to continue. Calling a friend requires finding the phone, deciding it is an appropriate hour, and accepting the uncertainty of the conversation. Finishing a project asks for access to notes, materials, attention, tolerable noise, and sometimes a way to begin without knowing exactly how the work will end.

This distinction has been measured in behavioral research. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in British Journal of Sports Medicine synthesized 25 independent samples from 22 papers, with 29,600 participants, examining physical-activity intention and behavior. Among those who intended to be active, researchers estimated an intention–behavior gap of 47.6%. That figure is specific to the studies and classification method: it does not mean 47.6% of all daily intentions fail, and it does not tell us why a particular person did or did not act. It tells us something more modest and important: intention is not sufficient on its own. [1]

A second meta-analysis, published in 2025, combined 92 studies and reported a moderate association between exercise intention and behavior, with an average correlation of r = 0.41. The included studies varied substantially. The reported relationship does not make intention irrelevant, but it shows how much remains unexplained by intention alone. [2]

These are studies about physical activity, not proof that every household task, creative effort or relationship follows the same numerical pattern. The cup in the sink is an invitation to consider a mechanism, not a statistical claim.

The environment is part of the verb

Some actions are hard because the physical world resists them. A chair cannot be moved easily. A door is too heavy. The bus stop is far away. A form asks for information the person cannot locate. Other actions are difficult because the environment makes the first step ambiguous. The necessary object is hidden, the task has no visible entry point, or the consequences of beginning are uncertain.

Design changes the shape of these situations. A visible handle suggests where to grasp. A prepared work surface can reduce the number of decisions before a task. A predictable routine can make it easier to know when an activity belongs. None of these modifications changes whether someone genuinely cares about the goal; they change what acting requires.

This is one reason the study of functioning cannot stop at individual motivation. A person may be able to perform a component movement in one setting yet find the full activity difficult somewhere else. Real action includes tools, obstacles, habits, opportunities, social relationships and the meaning the person attaches to the task.

That observation is compatible with occupational perspectives that view executive functioning both in terms of cognitive processes and in the performance of complex, goal-directed activities. A scoping review of the occupational-therapy literature noted that executive functioning is discussed in both ways rather than through a single simple definition. [3]

The point is not to replace a person's responsibilities with an explanation from the environment. It is to avoid treating the environment as an invisible, neutral background.

There is a moment when a goal needs a cue

Researchers studying behavior change distinguish having a goal from preparing for the moment at which the goal will be pursued. One studied approach is the implementation intention: a plan that connects a recognizable cue to an action. Instead of 'I will move more', a person might decide, 'When I finish lunch on Tuesday, I will take a ten-minute walk on the level path nearby.'

The cue matters because future situations are busy. An abstract wish competes poorly with everything already happening. A cue creates a particular time-and-place doorway through which the intention may pass.

A 2024 meta-analysis covering 642 independent tests found that if–then planning can improve several classes of outcomes, with results varying by behavior and study design. Effects were generally stronger when plans had a genuine contingent form, when people were motivated, and when plans were rehearsed. [4] This does not make planning a universal remedy. If an intended action is inaccessible, unsafe, or unsupported, a well-written plan cannot make the external obstacle vanish.

Notice how a useful cue changes the question. Rather than ask only, 'How badly do I want to do it?', we can ask, 'What will tell me that now is the moment to begin?' The second question is more concrete. It also invites observation and revision: perhaps the chosen cue never occurs, or the response takes more time than available.

Starting can be more difficult than continuing

A task has transitions. Before its first movement, there may be preparation, uncertainty and resistance; once started, objects and feedback become available. A sentence on a page gives the writer something to revise. A few arranged materials allow the next step to become visible. A small completed segment can change the perceived shape of the whole task.

This does not mean everyone should divide every activity into tiny pieces. Some activities lose their meaning when fragmented. Some need uninterrupted time or coordinated help. But the difference between starting and continuing is worth investigating rather than assuming the task's difficulty is uniform from beginning to end.

The role of feedback matters, too. People adjust action in response to what happens. A route proves unpleasant; a conversation turns out to be easier than expected; an unfamiliar tool feels awkward. An intention that survives encounter with reality often becomes a revised intention, not a perfect execution of the original plan.

Perhaps this is why we should be cautious when measuring success only as obedience to an initial decision. Good functioning can include changing one's plan intelligently. It can include stopping when a situation is unsafe, asking for assistance, or discovering that another activity matters more.

Habit is neither prison nor magic

Habits are often presented as a solution to the effort of deciding. Repeat an action often enough, the story goes, and it happens automatically. There is truth in the importance of learning and repetition, but the word habit can hide different phenomena: a familiar sequence, a cue-triggered response, a practiced skill, or an action that persists because circumstances are stable.

Neuroscience studies describe how cortical and basal-ganglia circuits participate in learned movement sequences. A 2025 research review examined how some motor-sequence processes become organized through circuits involving the dorsolateral striatum and related pathways. [5] Such work helps explain a part of skill learning, but it does not show that a person's everyday choices are programmed by a single structure.

A habit also inherits the environment in which it was learned. Change the hour, tools, route or social setting and an activity that once flowed may suddenly require effort. That observation complicates simple moral judgments about consistency. What looks like a character trait from the outside may partly depend on a remarkably stable arrangement of cues and supports.

At the same time, it is important not to romanticize automation. Some routines continue after they cease to help. To function well is not always to act without thinking; sometimes it is to interrupt the familiar sequence and choose again.

A person's reasons matter

Not every uncompleted intention should be repaired. A promise may have been made under pressure. A goal may belong to someone else's expectations. A task may demand a cost that is invisible to the observer. Repeated difficulty may prompt practical changes, but it may also require revisiting the goal itself.

This raises an ethical distinction between supporting an action and controlling a person. A well-designed prompt or environment can make a chosen activity easier. The same mechanism can also push people toward actions they did not freely choose. The ethics depends on who set the goal, whether alternatives remain available, and whether the person can decline.

In homes, classrooms, clinics and workplaces, we could therefore ask two separate questions: 'What would make this activity possible?' and 'Is this activity something the person actually wants or needs?' The first invites design; the second protects agency. Keeping them together prevents efficiency from becoming the only measure of success.

From a single decision to a landscape of action

Imagine drawing the distance between wanting and doing. It would not be a straight line. It might be a landscape with doors, ramps, delays, interruptions, invitations and shortcuts. Some routes are easy because they have been traveled many times. Others are difficult because the path is unfamiliar or the destination uncertain. Some routes are blocked, regardless of determination.

We could map this landscape by asking what happened just before a beginning. Was there a cue? A person nearby? A material ready to hand? Was the action small enough to fit the available time? Was the environment familiar? What made stopping possible? What changed after feedback arrived?

These are not questions that can be answered simply by measuring willpower. They require looking at the interaction among capacity, opportunity, social expectations, meaning and time. They also invite experimentation — not necessarily a laboratory experiment, but careful observation of a chosen routine and the conditions under which it becomes easier or harder.

We should resist turning the observations into universal formulas. Two people can face different obstacles even when an activity looks identical. The same person can experience different barriers on different days. The most informative unit may not be 'the type of person' but 'the person in this particular situation, at this particular moment.'

The meaningful first step

Return to the cup. Perhaps the person washed it the next morning. Perhaps someone else did. Perhaps the sink was inaccessible, the water was off, or the person decided to rest. Without knowing the context, the unfinished cup explains very little.

The interesting story does not begin with blame or with a heroic command to try harder. It begins with curiosity about what the action required and which of those requirements were met. An intention can be genuine and remain unrealized. An action can become possible because a tiny part of its setting changes. A sensible person can also abandon an intention when new evidence arrives.

Between wanting and doing there is no single empty space waiting to be crossed by force. There is a world. Learning to see that world may be more useful than repeating the instruction to begin.

Evidence and reading

[1] Feil, Fritsch & Rhodes (2023), The intention-behaviour gap in physical activity: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Twenty-five independent samples, 29,600 participants; findings apply to studied physical-activity contexts.

[2] Yang, Li & Liu (2025), Exercise intention and behavior meta-analysis. Ninety-two studies; heterogeneity and observational relationships limit simple causal interpretation.

[3] Cramm et al. (2013), Executive functioning: a scoping review of occupational therapy literature. Professional concepts, not an individual diagnostic framework.

[4] Implementation-intention meta-analysis (2024), The when and how of planning: 642 tests. Plans' effectiveness varies by context, task and implementation.

[5] How circuits for habits are formed within the basal ganglia (2025). Circuit-level research and review, not a direct explanation for every everyday action.
