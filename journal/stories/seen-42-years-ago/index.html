ARTICLE 021 · CREATIVE ANTIDOTE / FICTION

Seen 42 Years Ago

Collection: The Night Sky

Original fiction. This story and its characters are invented. Read the paired nonfiction article for the established research.

Original fiction. Characters and events are invented.

Aetherion Journal · Article 021 Creative Antidote
Category: Fiction / Young Adult / Science Fiction
Collection: The Night Sky
Relationship: Companion to Why the Night Sky Shows Us Different Moments in Time
Reading time: ~5 minutes

Original fiction. The alien message, planet and characters are invented. The underlying travel-time principle is real: radio waves travel at the speed of light in a vacuum.

At 9:41 on Saturday night, Leen was arguing with her friend Sami about whether a hot dog counted as a sandwich when the observatory announced that Earth had received a message from another world.

Sami read the headline twice.

“That's inconvenient,” he said. “We haven't settled the sandwich question.”

The message had arrived as a pattern of radio pulses from a fictional planet the astronomers called Morrow, forty-two light-years away. It took researchers days to agree on a possible translation. At last, on a giant screen in the town square, four words appeared:

WHAT DOES HOME SOUND LIKE?

The crowd went silent.

Then everybody began talking at once.

“Send our national anthem!”

“Send a speech!”

“Send proof we're intelligent!”

Sami looked at Leen. “Maybe don't send our group chat.”

An astronomer climbed the steps to the microphone.

“There's something else,” she said. “The signal left Morrow roughly forty-two years ago. In 1984.”

Leen did the math in her head.

“So whoever asked that question has been waiting since before I was born?”

“They haven't necessarily been waiting,” the astronomer said. “They might have forgotten they sent it. They might have moved away. We don't know.”

“And if we answer tonight?”

“They could receive our reply in 2068. If they answer immediately, we wouldn't receive that answer until about 2110.”

Sami held up his phone. “Worst group chat in the universe.”

By morning, the town council had formed a Committee for Interplanetary First Impressions.

By lunchtime, the committee had chosen a slogan: HUMANITY: PLEASE FIND OUR PRESENTATION ATTACHED.

By afternoon, it had a ninety-three-slide presentation.

Leen watched a rehearsal in which the mayor explained transportation, agriculture, music, and the excellent quality of the new parking garage.

At slide fifty-seven, she slipped outside.

Her grandmother was sitting on the balcony, sorting old recordings on her phone.

“Do you think we should tell them something important?” Leen asked.

Her grandmother pressed play. There was a hiss, then the voice of Leen's late grandfather laughing because somebody had dropped a pan.

“I saved this because I knew the laugh would go away someday,” she said. “And then I discovered it hadn't.”

Leen listened again.

In the recording, a kettle whistled. A window rattled. Someone said, “The bread is burning.”

Her grandmother smiled. “That's what our home sounded like.”

The next day Leen and Sami went around town collecting sounds—with permission from the people they recorded.

A bicycle bell. A ball striking the pavement. A spoon tapping the side of a glass. Two cousins laughing so hard they couldn't finish a sentence. A market vendor calling out a price. Rain on a metal awning. A grandmother explaining, for the tenth time, that the bread was not burned, it was well done.

Sami added twelve seconds of silence.

“What's that for?” Leen asked.

“For the people who need a minute.”

They submitted the recording to the committee.

The mayor listened with his eyes closed.

“Where,” he asked finally, “are the impressive achievements?”

Leen pointed at the speaker.

“All over it.”

There was a long argument. The mayor wanted the parking garage included. Sami suggested adding its automatic ticket machine, which sounded like a disappointed robot. Somehow that helped.

By evening, the committee had voted to transmit a short greeting and a selection of the town's ordinary sounds.

Not because the sounds represented everyone on Earth. They didn't.

Because the question had asked about home, and this was one honest answer from one place.

On the observatory roof, a technician aimed the transmitting antenna toward Morrow's expected position.

Leen stood beside Sami, watching a green indicator light.

“Ready?” the technician asked.

Leen imagined the message traveling past the Moon, beyond the outer planets, into a distance no one in the town could cross.

She imagined it arriving forty-two years later, in a place whose streets and languages and people might have changed completely.

Perhaps the person who asked the question would hear it.

Perhaps their granddaughter would.

Perhaps nobody would.

“Ready,” Leen said.

The technician pressed SEND.

Nothing dramatic happened. No beam split the sky. No alien face appeared above the town.

The antenna continued its quiet work.

Sami opened the messaging app on his phone and typed a new name for their conversation: MORROW — DON'T EXPECT A QUICK REPLY.

“Can we at least mark the message as delivered?” he asked.

“Not for forty-two years,” Leen said.

He stared at the stars.

“Fine. But if they reply with just ‘k,’ I'm leaving the group.”

Leen laughed. Then she listened to the town behind them: traffic, distant voices, a dog objecting to something invisible.

For the first time, those sounds seemed worth sending across the universe.

THE END

Fiction and fact

Established science: Light and radio waves are electromagnetic radiation. In a vacuum, they travel at the speed of light; for a source about 42 light-years away, a signal takes about 42 years to arrive. A reply would take about another 42 years, assuming an approximately fixed distance and ignoring small complications.

Invented for the story: The planet Morrow, the received message, the translation, the observatory's transmission, and all characters. There is no claim that extraterrestrial life has sent this message.

Reader prompt: If you could send just ten seconds of everyday sound to someone forty-two light-years away, what would you choose—and why?
