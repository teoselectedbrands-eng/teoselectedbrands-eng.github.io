The Recipe That Needed a Passport

Collection: Food, Memory & Migration

Original fiction. This story and its characters are invented. Read the paired nonfiction article for the established research.

Original fiction. Characters and events are invented.

Aetherion Journal — Creative Antidote 022
Genre: Original fantasy satire / short fiction
Audience: Teenagers and adults
Companion to: Article 022, A Recipe Has More Than One Home

At the Border Between Kitchens, everything had to declare its origin.

The line began at a cupboard in one house and ended at the stove in another. Along the way stood a little customs booth with a kettle, a clock that ran five minutes slow, and a sign:

NO UNDECLARED SPICES. NO UNVERIFIED GRANDMOTHERS.

At eight on a rainy Tuesday morning, a recipe arrived carrying a suitcase that smelled of garlic.

“Passport,” said the officer.

The recipe unfolded itself. It was written on a yellow card, softened at the corners and stained with something that might have been tomato. Across the top, in blue ink, were the words Sunday Pot. Beneath them, five different people had added corrections.

“I’m afraid I haven’t got a passport,” it said. “Only instructions.”

“Country of origin?”

“A kitchen near the sea.”

“Which country?”

“The one with the crooked window and the chair nobody was allowed to sit on.”

The officer rubbed his forehead.

“Ingredients?”

“Tomatoes, lentils, onions, herbs, and whatever somebody can find when the shops are closed.”

“‘Whatever’ is not an ingredient.”

“Try telling that to a hungry family.”

The officer stamped a red rectangle on the counter.

REFER TO THE DEPARTMENT OF AUTHENTICITY.

The Department of Authenticity occupied a building shaped like a measuring cup.

Its director, Madam Exact, wore a white coat and a necklace of tiny silver teaspoons. She placed the recipe under a machine labeled THE ORIGINAL-O-METER.

“This device,” she announced, “will identify the one true version of you.”

“Excellent,” said the recipe. “I’ve been trying to figure that out for years.”

The machine whirred.

ERROR: THREE HANDWRITINGS.

It whirred again.

ERROR: TWO DIFFERENT COOKING TIMES.

A third time.

ERROR: SOMEONE HAS WRITTEN ‘A LITTLE MORE LEMON’ WITHOUT SPECIFYING HOW MUCH.

Madam Exact looked horrified.

“Who allowed this?”

“An aunt,” said the recipe. “Then a cousin. Then a teenager who said it needed more lemon and refused to apologize.”

“Changes!” cried Madam Exact. “You have been changed!”

“Of course,” said the recipe. “I’ve been cooked.”

Madam Exact turned a dial to PURITY.

The machine began removing everything it judged to be a later addition. It erased a pinch of herb. It crossed out a note about cooking on a smaller stove. It deleted the sentence Call Aunt Noura if it doesn't smell right.

The card grew thinner and thinner until almost nothing remained except:

Put something in a pot. Heat.

Madam Exact lifted the card triumphantly.

“There. The original.”

The recipe stared at itself.

“That,” it whispered, “doesn’t taste like anything.”

Just then, someone knocked on the measuring-cup door.

A girl of sixteen pushed her way inside. Her name was Salma, and she was holding a shopping bag with a torn handle.

“I'm here for the Sunday Pot,” she said. “My mother said it might be delayed at customs.”

“Are you the owner?” Madam Exact demanded.

“No. I'm the next cook.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

Salma looked at the nearly blank card and went very quiet.

“Where are the notes?”

“Removed,” said Madam Exact. “They compromised its authenticity.”

Salma reached into her bag and pulled out a phone. On the screen was a photograph of the old recipe, full of crossed-out words and handwriting in different colors.

“The first version my family knows was cooked by my great-grandmother,” she said. “We know her name. We know where she lived. Nobody’s trying to erase that.”

She zoomed in on a line near the bottom.

If you make it somewhere else, write down what you changed.

“That's her handwriting,” Salma said. “She left room.”

Madam Exact considered the sentence as though it were an extremely dangerous spice.

“If you change it, how can it still be the same recipe?”

Salma thought for a moment.

“My brother has grown taller since last summer. We still recognize him.”

“People are not recipes.”

“No,” said Salma. “He’s much harder to cook.”

For the first time in thirty years, Madam Exact laughed. It sounded like a teaspoon striking a glass.

They moved the hearing to the test kitchen.

Salma cooked the pot as her family remembered it. She used the photograph to restore the annotations and added one note of her own: The lemons here are sharper. Start with less.

The customs officer tasted it. Madam Exact tasted it. Even the Original-O-Meter tasted it, although no one had installed that feature.

A green light appeared.

RESULT: RECOGNIZABLE. NOT IDENTICAL.

Madam Exact sat down.

“So what am I supposed to stamp?” she asked.

The recipe, now warm and fragrant again, pointed to the space beneath its title.

“Try writing where I came from,” it said. “And leave space for where I go.”

Madam Exact picked up the stamp she almost never used.

HISTORY ACKNOWLEDGED. CHANGES RECORDED. TRAVEL PERMITTED.

At the Border Between Kitchens, the line began moving again.

A soup with no fixed quantities waved from a suitcase. A pastry carried six conflicting family instructions. A pickling recipe had brought a folder of legal documents and still smelled suspiciously of vinegar.

Salma took the Sunday Pot home.

That evening, she wrote a new line on the card:

October 31. Smaller stove. Less lemon. Still good.

Then, after a pause, she added:

Next cook: tell us what you discover.

THE END

Reader's doorway

What is real: Recipes can be passed down, adapted, and documented across generations and places; historical provenance and living variation are distinct questions explored in the companion nonfiction article.

What is invented: The Border Between Kitchens, Madam Exact, the Original-O-Meter, Salma, the Sunday Pot, and all events and dialogue are fictional. No claim is made that this recipe represents a particular traditional dish.

One question: If a recipe changed every time someone made it, what would you want future cooks to preserve: the ingredients, the method, the name, the story—or something else?
