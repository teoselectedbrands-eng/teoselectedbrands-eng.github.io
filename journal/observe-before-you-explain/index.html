AETHERION JOURNAL

Observe before you explain

The player pauses. Is it hesitation, timing or something the camera cannot show? One fictional moment gives us several possible readings.

A pause before a pass.

Imagine a short clip: a player stops for two seconds, looks across the court and then receives a pass. A note says, “The player lacked confidence.” Another says, “The player waited for space.” Both supply explanations that the visible sequence alone cannot decide between.

What is providedWhat a reader might inferWhat is missing
A two-second pause before the pass.The player was uncertain.Their account of the moment.
A look across the court.The player was checking a teammate.What was outside the frame.
The sequence then continued.The pause helped the play.The team’s goal and instructions.

The point is not to ban interpretation. An explanation becomes useful when we can see the information supporting it and what might change our view.

Write the note in two passes.

First, describe an action, direction, timing or position. Then add possible meanings on separate lines. Give each explanation one piece of missing context that would help you inspect it.

A drawing, a fictional description or permitted footage is enough. You do not need to watch strangers or turn a conversation into an assessment.

Try a better next question.

“What happened just before the clip?” asks for context. “Why was the player anxious?” already builds one explanation into the question. A participant’s own account may add something no camera angle provides, but asking should have a clear purpose and respect their choice not to answer.

The finished note can remain modest: “I saw a pause and a change of gaze. Several explanations fit. I would need the wider sequence before deciding what to ask next.”

This is an original note-making framework for learning. It has not been presented as a diagnostic, scouting or performance-rating tool.
