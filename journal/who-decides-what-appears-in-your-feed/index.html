ARTICLE 024 · RESEARCH / NONFICTION

Who Decides What Appears in Your Feed?

Collection: The Invisible Internet

Evidence-based nonfiction. Scientific sources and limitations are provided within the article.

Two people open the same app. They might as well have opened two different doors.

Imagine two friends sitting beside each other after school. One opens a video app and sees astronomy clips, cake decorating, a football highlight, and a person restoring an old camera. The other opens the same app and finds comedy sketches, study tips, a mysterious sea creature, and six variations of the same song.

Neither friend asked for that exact list.

So who arranged it?

The answer is more interesting than “the algorithm knows you.” Recommendation systems make predictions about what a person might want to see, using signals from the person, the content, and sometimes the wider platform. But a prediction is not a complete description of a human being.

1. Your feed is an edited selection, not the whole internet

A social-media platform may have far more posts and videos than any one person could see. Its recommendation systems first identify eligible candidates, then rank some of them for particular surfaces: a home feed, short-video stream, search page, or “Up Next” list.

The systems are not identical. TikTok says its For You recommendations consider interactions, information about videos, and user information. YouTube describes signals including viewing and search history, subscriptions, likes, dislikes, and satisfaction surveys. Instagram describes ranking predictions such as the likelihood that a person will comment, like, save, or otherwise interact with a post.

What appears on a screen can also be affected by platform rules, content eligibility, safety measures, and the specific feature being used. And advertisements, where present, may be selected through a related but separate process.

This is why the word algorithm can hide a surprising amount of complexity. There is not one universal machine making every decision in the same way everywhere.

2. Even your silence can be a signal

Imagine pausing on a video about antique watches. You do not like it. You do not share it. You simply watch to the end because someone is opening a tiny mechanism and you want to see what happens.

For some systems, watching time or completing a video can help predict interest. TikTok explicitly lists watching in full, skipping, and time spent watching among its recommendation signals.

But what did your pause mean? Curiosity? Confusion? Admiration? Disbelief? Accidentally leaving the phone on a table?

A recommendation system can detect some behavior; it cannot directly read every reason behind it. That gap matters. Your behavior supplies clues, not a perfect autobiography.

3. The prediction can change the next choice

Suppose you watch one video about a rare instrument. Similar videos appear. You watch another, discover a musician, and decide to learn more.

Here, the recommendations helped you find something you genuinely enjoyed.

Now imagine a different pattern. A single accidental click leads to many similar suggestions, until the feed feels narrower than your actual interests.

Both situations are plausible. Platforms describe efforts to introduce variety, but relevance and discovery do not always pull in the same direction.

This creates a feedback loop: your actions influence what you are offered, and the things you are offered can influence what you choose next.

That does not mean a feed entirely controls what you think. People search, question, ignore, follow friends, and move between platforms and offline experiences. The effects of recommendation systems depend on the design, the person, the content, and the timescale.

4. What has research actually found?

One major study published in Science in 2023 compared algorithmically ranked Facebook and Instagram feeds with reverse-chronological feeds among consenting users during the 2020 U.S. election. The change affected what participants saw and reduced time and activity on the platforms. Yet during the three-month study period, the researchers did not detect significant changes in several measured political attitudes, including polarization.

A smaller 2025 TikTok study followed 88 participants who experienced a less-personalized feed for a week. They used the service less and reported greater perceived control, but also found it less enjoyable.

These studies do not settle every question about algorithms. Their designs, platforms, participants, durations, and outcomes differ. They do, however, challenge two overly simple stories: that personalized feeds have no consequences, and that they necessarily determine people's beliefs.

5. Can you influence what appears?

Yes, to a degree—and the available controls vary by platform, region, account, and time.

Examples described in platforms' own guidance include marking a post “Not interested,” adjusting or refreshing recommendation preferences, managing watch history, unfollowing accounts, using keyword filters, and choosing a Following-style feed where offered.

In the European Union, the Digital Services Act requires designated very large online platforms using recommender systems to offer at least one option for each such system that is not based on profiling. That is a regional legal requirement, not proof that every user everywhere sees the same options.

Controls are useful, but none makes a feed identical to the entire internet—or guarantees an unbiased view of the world.

6. An experiment in deliberate curiosity

Try this without sharing personal information or changing any important privacy settings:

First, write down three things your current feed seems to assume you enjoy.

Next, deliberately search for something you have not been offered recently: an unfamiliar art form, a scientific question, a language you are learning, or a subject suggested by someone you trust. Open more than one reliable source if the topic involves factual claims.

Finally, ask: Did the feed reflect an interest I already had? Did it introduce a genuinely new one? What would I never have found if I only kept scrolling?

You are not conducting a scientific experiment. You are observing the difference between being recommended something and choosing to explore something.

The feed is a doorway, not a map of your mind

A recommended post may be funny, useful, moving, or unexpected. Sometimes it takes you somewhere you are glad to have discovered.

But your interests are bigger than a sequence of predicted clicks.

There are books you have not heard of, questions you have not asked, places you have not imagined, and people whose work has never reached your screen.

Perhaps the most valuable button is not always Next.

Sometimes it is Search.

Evidence boundaries: The opening friends and the antique-watch example are invented illustrations, not accounts of observed people. Platform descriptions explain their publicly reported systems, not independently audited, complete source code; mechanisms may change. The research findings are context- and time-dependent and do not establish universal causal effects for all users, especially minors or non-U.S. populations. Recommendations and paid advertisements should not be conflated. This is not a tutorial for any specific current app interface.
