A Recipe Has More Than One Home

Collection: Food, Memory & Migration

Evidence-based nonfiction. Scientific sources and limitations are provided within the article.

What happens to a recipe when the people who know it move away?

The recipe says: Two lemons.

The person making it says: “That depends.”

The handwriting belongs to an older relative. The paper is stained at the edges. Someone has added a note in a different pen: Less salt here. And beneath it, almost as an afterthought: Ask your aunt about the oven.

This is an imagined recipe card, not an archival discovery. Yet it captures a familiar puzzle. A recipe can look fixed on paper and remain wonderfully changeable in real life.

How can one dish belong to a family, a neighborhood, a country—and sometimes a kitchen thousands of kilometers away?

An ingredient can have a passport older than the dish

Take the tomato.

Today it is difficult to imagine many Mediterranean meals without it: fresh in salads, softened into sauces, or cooked slowly with other ingredients. But the tomato's history began in the Americas, not around the Mediterranean Sea.

The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations describes its South American origins and its use in Mesoamerica before it became established in European cooking. Its journey to Europe was connected to the era of Spanish conquest and transatlantic exchange, and historians do not reduce its spread to a single universally agreed route. It became a widely popular Mediterranean cooking ingredient much later.

Think about what that means. An ingredient can arrive from somewhere else, become familiar over generations, and eventually feel inseparable from local cuisine.

That does not erase the place where the ingredient originated. Nor does it mean every later dish is an imitation. It means food histories contain movement, adaptation, power, and time.

The knowledge that never fits on the card

A recipe might tell you to knead for five minutes. But how should the dough feel?

It might instruct you to cook something until ready. What does ready smell like? What sound does a pan make just before a cook lowers the heat?

Culinary knowledge often travels through gestures and observation as much as through measurements. Food historian Anny Gaul, writing about Moroccan kitchen histories, notes that important aspects of culinary practice survive in learned gestures, oral traditions, and memories rather than conventional written archives alone.

A page can preserve ingredients. A person can show you how to use them.

Sometimes, of course, a page is all that remains—and it becomes precious for that reason.

When the kitchen moves

Migration can change the conditions in which a familiar meal is made.

Perhaps a particular herb is unavailable, a different oven changes the timing, or a household has less time to prepare food. Sometimes an ingredient is substituted out of necessity. Sometimes a change is a deliberate new preference. Sometimes a family insists on preserving one detail exactly.

The Canadian Museum of Immigration at Pier 21 describes how cookbooks can help migrant communities preserve connections while also revealing disruption: knowledge once shared through time together in a kitchen may need to be learned from written instructions in a new place. Cookbooks can record both continuity and adaptation.

In a 2019 study of Lebanese food and the Lebanese diaspora in London, Ali Abdallah, Thomas Fletcher, and Kevin Hannam examined how food practices related to belonging and cultural identity. Their research described ways that Lebanese food culture abroad could be maintained, adapted, hybridized, and contested. There was no single simple story of either preservation or loss.

A changed dish, then, is not automatically a forgotten one. But not every change needs a grand explanation, either. Sometimes the cook simply likes it better this way.

Who decides what is authentic?

Imagine three people describing the “proper” way to prepare a familiar dish.

One recalls how their grandmother made it. Another points to a respected cookbook. The third remembers the version sold in the neighborhood where they grew up.

They might all be telling the truth about their experiences. But those experiences alone may not settle the dish's documented origins or every claim made about its history.

The word authentic can express care for a tradition. It can also become a way of dismissing the lives and adaptations of people who carry that tradition elsewhere.

A better conversation might begin with more precise questions: Which version? From which place? At what time? Learned from whom? Changed for what reason?

Recognizing variation does not require pretending that every historical origin claim is equally supported. Research still matters. So do the people whose knowledge has too often gone unrecorded.

A meal is more than its ingredients

UNESCO's account of Mediterranean food heritage extends beyond a list of dishes. It describes skills associated with cultivation, processing, cooking, sharing, hospitality, and the transmission of knowledge. Meals can be occasions for social exchange and the renewal of relationships.

This helps explain why a recipe can matter even when nobody is hungry.

A particular smell may remind someone of a place. A repeated gesture may recall a person. A small alteration can mark the moment a household adapted to a different life.

None of these memories proves a historical origin. But they are part of the human story surrounding the food.

The margin-note experiment

Find a recipe that exists in at least two versions. It could come from a family, a friend, a cookbook, or a community kitchen.

Compare the details. Then ask—not to find a winner, but to discover a history:

Who taught you this version? What changed? What would you never change?

Record the answers with permission. Keep the disagreements. They may reveal more than a single polished recipe ever could.

A recipe can begin somewhere specific and travel into many lives. It can preserve a memory while leaving room for a new one.

And perhaps the most interesting line on the old recipe card is the one that does not describe an ingredient at all:

Ask your aunt about the oven.

Evidence boundaries: The recipe card and dialogue at the opening are explicitly imagined. FAO's tomato history does not establish the origins of any particular modern dish. UNESCO describes an intangible-cultural-heritage practice rather than testing a causal claim. The Lebanese and Moroccan studies are context-specific qualitative research and should not be generalized to all migrants or cuisines. The history of food exchange also includes conquest and unequal power, which should not be romanticized.
