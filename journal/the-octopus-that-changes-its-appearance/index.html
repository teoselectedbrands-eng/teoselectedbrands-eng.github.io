ARTICLE 026 · RESEARCH / NONFICTION

The Octopus That Changes Its Appearance

Collection: Hidden Lives of the Ocean

Evidence-based nonfiction. Scientific sources and limitations are provided within the article.

An animal can become patterned like a reef in seconds. The greater mystery is how it knows what pattern to make.

Imagine looking at a patch of underwater rock. You see pale sand, dark cracks, and a cluster of rough brown stones. Then one of the stones blinks.

An arm unfolds. Another follows. What seemed to be part of the reef turns into an octopus—and in a moment its skin begins to look different again.

This is not a transformation invented for a film. Octopuses really can change the visible pattern, brightness, and texture of their bodies with extraordinary speed. To understand how, we must look beneath the surface of their skin—and reconsider what it means to see a world.

1. A living display made of tiny pigment organs

An octopus does not change appearance by growing a new coat of pigment every time it moves. Much of the rapid change comes from structures called chromatophores.

Each chromatophore contains a tiny pigment sac surrounded by radial muscles. Nerve signals cause the muscles to contract, spreading the sac and making the colored area more visible. When the muscles relax, the sac becomes smaller. In enormous combinations, these structures create mottles, patches, and other patterns.

It is tempting to compare chromatophores to pixels on a screen, but they are living organs, controlled by muscles and nerves rather than electrical display software. Some changes can be astonishingly fast: research on cephalopods describes chromatophore expansion on the order of a tenth of a second.

Not every species uses the same colors or patterns. An octopus's appearance is shaped by the repertoire its species has evolved.

2. Color is only part of the disguise

A reef is not a flat photograph. It has bumps, ridges, holes, shadows, and pieces of seaweed that move in the water.

Some octopuses can alter their skin's texture using muscular projections called papillae. Their surface can appear comparatively smooth and then become uneven or pointed, helping disrupt the body's outline against a rough background.

Researchers have investigated the neural and muscular mechanisms of papillae in cuttlefish, close cephalopod relatives that share this remarkable ability with octopuses. These studies clarify a biological mechanism, but details demonstrated in cuttlefish should not automatically be treated as measured in every octopus species.

Reflecting skin structures also affect appearance. Chromatophores contain pigment, while structures such as iridophores and leucophores interact with the light that reaches or reflects from the animal. The combination can produce effects that pigment alone cannot explain.

An octopus's disguise is therefore a coordinated change in pattern, brightness, reflected light, body shape, and texture—not just a switch from one color to another.

3. The surprise: the color-changing animal may not see color as we do

This is where the story gets stranger.

Most octopuses studied have only one main class of visual pigment in their eyes. That suggests they cannot distinguish colors in the familiar human way, which relies on comparing signals from different cone types.

Yet some octopuses seem remarkably successful at matching the appearance of backgrounds. A 2022 study examined the reflected light from the skin of Octopus tetricus and compared it with algae and sponges. The researchers found that the animal could match certain background colors from the perspective of some potential predators, though not all colors or visual systems equally well.

How can an animal with such limited conventional color discrimination achieve that result?

Scientists are still working on the explanation. Matching brightness, contrast, texture, and other visual features may account for much of the camouflage. Proposed additional ways of extracting color information remain under investigation.

The octopus does not need to recreate its surroundings perfectly for every viewer. It needs to be difficult for a predator—or prey—to detect in the conditions that matter.

4. Could the skin itself sense light?

In 2015, researchers studying the California two-spot octopus (Octopus bimaculoides) reported something unusual: isolated samples of its skin responded to light by expanding chromatophores, even without input from the eyes or central nervous system.

The researchers also identified light-sensitive proteins associated with that response. They called it light-activated chromatophore expansion, or LACE.

This is fascinating, but it needs careful interpretation.

It does not show that an octopus's skin forms pictures the way its eyes do. Nor does it prove exactly how this local light response contributes to camouflage in a freely moving animal. Vision and brain-driven neural commands remain central to the established explanation of rapid body patterning.

A biological system can respond to light without having anything resembling human sight.

5. Disappearing has a cost

Camouflage can look effortless in a short underwater video. It is not necessarily cheap.

In research published in 2024, Sofie Sonner and Kirt Onthank measured oxygen consumption in skin samples from ruby octopuses while their chromatophores were activated. They used the measurements to model how costly widespread activation might be for the animal.

Their estimate suggested that a fully activated chromatophore system could require energy approaching the octopus's resting metabolic demand.

That is a striking finding, but it is an estimate based on skin measurements and a model, not a direct measurement of the energy used by an entire octopus every time it changes color in the ocean.

Even so, it changes how we might view the performance: the living display is supported by physiological work.

6. Is camouflage a decision?

A video of an octopus shifting its appearance invites an easy story: It looked at the reef, chose a disguise, and became a rock.

That story is memorable, but it slips quickly from observation into assumptions about the animal's thoughts.

We can observe its behavior and identify some neural mechanisms. We can test which backgrounds influence camouflage, and compare patterns across situations. But we cannot read an octopus's private intentions from a color change alone.

Cephalopod researchers describe complex connections between visual processing and patterns expressed on the skin, while acknowledging major unanswered questions about how the brain organizes those transformations.

The mystery is not diminished when we admit what remains unknown. It becomes more interesting.

7. Try looking twice

Find a clear photograph of an octopus resting against a reef. Before reading its caption, take a moment to identify the animal's outline.

Notice the relationship between the animal and the scene: light patches, dark edges, rough surfaces, and the visual clutter behind it.

Then imagine being a fish approaching from a different angle, with a different visual system. Would the same pattern work just as well?

This is a thought exercise, not an experiment. But it reveals why camouflage is more than a question of whether we can spot the animal in a photograph.

An octopus does not literally turn into a rock. Its skin and body produce signals that can make it harder to distinguish from its surroundings.

And the most astonishing part may not be how much its appearance changes—but how much biology is involved in making that change possible.

Evidence boundaries: The opening reef encounter is an illustrative scene, not an observed event. Findings may differ across octopus species; some comparisons and mechanisms derive from broader cephalopod studies. The article does not make claims about octopus intelligence beyond the visual and neural processes described.
