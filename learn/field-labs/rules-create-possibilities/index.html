Track 04 · Play & Participation · Self-paced scenario

Change the rule. Change the possibilities.

A fictional paper-bridge group experiment about roles, spoken and nonverbal contribution, choice and the limits of simple inclusion claims.

Independent learning sample, approximately 15–20 minutes. No enrolment, tests, certificates, individual assessment, or submissions. The full Rules Create Possibilities course remains a curriculum outline.

  1. 01 Notice the rules
  2. 02 Change one rule
  3. 03 Study participation
  4. 04 Facilitate and reflect

01 / Notice

Four people. One paper bridge challenge.

Imagine a fictional group of four trying to build a bridge from sheets of paper that can hold a small toy. The goal is to explore cooperation and design. It is not a speed competition or a test of anyone's abilities.

Rule set A

One person controls the paper.

  • One designated builder may touch the materials.
  • Other group members may only offer spoken instructions.
  • There is a short countdown.
Observation lens

Who can contribute, and how?

  • The builder handles all objects.
  • Some people may prefer writing or demonstrating.
  • The time limit could change how ideas are considered.
Your first question

Which contribution modes does Rule set A permit? Which does it restrict? Do not label a participant as disengaged when the rule itself narrows their options.

Reveal an example observation versus inference

Observation: only the designated builder handled paper.

Inference to test: someone who did not speak may have had no useful idea. That conclusion is not supported by silence alone, especially when speaking was the only permitted contribution channel.

02 / Change

Keep the goal; rewrite one rule.

Now keep the same paper bridge goal and materials but replace the contribution rule: anyone may participate through speaking, sketching, arranging materials or observing and offering feedback. Roles may change by agreement.

Before

One builderSpeaking onlyCountdown

What opportunities are built into the rule? What participation is excluded by the rule?

After the rule change

Shared materialsChoice of contributionNegotiated roles

More contribution formats are possible, but that does not guarantee they will all be used or valued.

Important: A rule change may affect coordination, time or conflict. Inclusion is not just maximizing options; it requires noticing how decisions, materials and group expectations actually work together.

Reveal the hidden trade-off

Opening access to the materials can allow more ways to contribute but may also introduce crowding or role confusion. A group might agree to rotate materials, provide a clear sketching station, or invite a timekeeper. The best design must be examined in the actual context.

03 / Compare participation

What should we actually observe?

Separate rule design from a claim about its effect
DimensionRule set ARule set B
Who may handle materials?One designated builderMembers may negotiate who handles them
What counts as contributing?Spoken advice or buildingSpeaking, sketching, building or reflective feedback
What could become difficult?Silence misread as disinterestRoles, materials or turns may become unclear
What we cannot inferThat group members dislike the activityThat everyone now feels included
Access

Were options usable?

Could people use the available contribution formats without penalty or unwanted attention?

Interaction

Were ideas considered?

Notice how a group's choices respond to spoken, sketched and demonstrated suggestions.

Meaning

What mattered to people?

Ask what contributors value rather than assuming participation can be inferred from the amount of speech.

Read a cautious conclusion

“Rule set B permits more contribution formats on paper. We have not observed a real group, so we cannot conclude that it improves participation outcomes. The next step would be to try the activity voluntarily and gather respectful, appropriately consented feedback.”

04 / Facilitate

Design the invitation, not a forced performance.

Imagine facilitating the same fictional activity. Start by explaining the shared purpose. Offer more than one way to join. Allow a person to observe without public justification and agree how changes will be negotiated. Close with a question instead of ranking who contributed most.

Invite

“Here are several ways to participate.”

Notice

“Is anyone's chosen role unavailable because of our rules?”

Reflect

“What should we keep or change next time?”

On-paper facilitator checklist
  • What is the activity actually for?
  • Can people contribute in different ways?
  • Are materials, space and timing manageable?
  • Can someone change role or decline participation?
  • What evidence would support a claim that the change helped?
Your design challenge

Choose one rule to change again—materials, time, role rotation or how feedback is shared. Explain one benefit you hope for, one possible side effect and how you would know the difference. No correct single answer is assumed.

This illustration is an educational design exercise. It is not a standardised developmental, clinical or educational evaluation, and it does not recommend testing children without appropriate safeguards.

Keep exploring the system.

A rule changes the activity, the roles and sometimes the meaning of participation. Keep following the question.
