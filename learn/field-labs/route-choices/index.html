Track 03 · Place & Environment · Self-paced scenario

Two routes. One destination. Many questions.

A paper-based library route comparison that explores distance, passage conditions, signage and real-world uncertainty.

Independent learning sample, approximately 15–20 minutes. No enrolment, tests, certificates, individual assessment, or submissions. The full Routes, Access & Choice course remains a curriculum outline.

  1. 01 Observe the route
  2. 02 Compare possibilities
  3. 03 Test assumptions
  4. 04 Redesign and reflect

01 / Observe

Two paths to the same reading room.

A fictional community library has two routes from its entrance to a reading room. One appears more direct. The other goes around a shared corridor. Neither path can be declared the better choice from its shape alone.

Make two observations.

Which route has fewer turns on the drawing? Which route includes a marked narrower doorway? Keep an observation separate from a statement about who can or cannot use either route.

Reveal what the illustration actually establishes

Route A is drawn as more direct and includes a narrower doorway. Route B is drawn as longer and passes through a shared corridor. Exact slopes, widths, lighting, noise and individual needs are not established.

02 / Compare

Shorter is not automatically easier.

Route A: direct path

  • Fewer turns in the schematic
  • Narrower doorway is marked
  • Potential passage bottleneck
  • Actual usability still unknown

Route B: shared corridor

  • Longer path and more turns
  • Shared movement space
  • Wayfinding sign may be unclear
  • Actual usability still unknown

03 / Investigate

What question would change your decision?

A visitor arrives with a large, rigid art portfolio. Another visitor is unfamiliar with the building. We cannot infer either person's preferred route. The purpose of a design audit is to test assumptions with suitable evidence and actual use—not assign people to paths based on labels.

Physical

What measurements matter?

Door operation, usable passage width, turning conditions and route obstructions.

Information

How is the route explained?

Signs, instructions, landmarks, language and alternative navigation methods.

Experience

Whose feedback matters?

People who use the space, staff who maintain it, and those encountering it for the first time.

Reveal a cautious decision statement

“The direct route may be faster for some journeys, but the doorway could limit its usability. The longer route may offer an alternative, but unclear signs could increase confusion. We need real measurements and user feedback.” This statement separates possibilities from verified findings.

04 / Redesign

Improve choice, then recheck the whole system.

Propose three changes on paper: improve the sign to Route B, move a temporary object away from Route A, and mark where a visitor can ask for help. Each change has a maintenance responsibility and may affect shared space.

Change

Improve the route information at the entrance.

Recheck

Can people understand the alternative without special knowledge?

Learn

Compare visitors' actual navigation, not merely the design team's intention.

Your field note

Sketch a third approach that preserves clear choices for different users. Record one improvement hypothesis, one possible new disadvantage and one observation you would need before making a claim.

See an example feedback loop

Hypothesis: clearer signs may reduce wrong turns. Trade-off: a new sign could clutter the entrance or be placed out of view. Evidence: ask participants to find the reading room using the actual proposed signage, while noting the study's scope and limitations.

A fictional teaching illustration cannot establish compliance with building codes, accessibility standards or professional site assessment requirements.

A question leads somewhere else.

Notice the trade-offs; keep what the scenario helps you question, not an untested conclusion.
