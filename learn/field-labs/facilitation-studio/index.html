Track 07 · Facilitation & Community · Open learning sample

Ten lenses. One room. A thousand design questions.

Observe before interpreting. Give things precise names. Design a workshop for real choices, examine what happens, and revise the plan—without claiming you can read anyone's mind.

Independent fictional learning exercise, about 20–30 minutes. This is a studio sample related to the How a Workshop Works curriculum outline. It is not a completed course, professional training qualification, booked workshop or assessment. No sign-in or response submission.

01 / Observation

Ten lenses; not ten judgments.

The fictional scenario: six adults arrive for a 20-minute learning encounter about a paper-bridge challenge. There is a shared table, several chairs, some background noise, and varied preferences for talking, sketching, making or listening. A facilitator sees two people talking, one drawing quietly and a person standing near the door. None of these actions reveals a diagnosis, motivation or emotion.

Observation lens means a question that directs attention to a particular part of the situation. It is not a test of the person being observed.

LENS 01Purpose

What is the shared activity for?

LENS 02Action

What can be directly described?

LENS 03Space

What routes and positions exist?

LENS 04Objects

Which materials shape choices?

LENS 05Time

What pace and transitions matter?

LENS 06Sensory context

What is the light, sound or crowding?

LENS 07People & roles

Who can contribute and decide?

LENS 08Choice

Can people participate differently?

LENS 09Evidence

How would we check a claim?

LENS 10Uncertainty

What do we not yet know?

Try the ten-lens exercise

Choose any three lenses. Write one neutral observation for the fictional scene, one question about context, and one thing you cannot determine. The task is to slow down unjustified conclusions—not to collect private information about real strangers.

Reveal a careful observation example

Observable: One participant is making marks on a sheet of paper while two others are speaking.

Inference: “The quiet participant does not want to join.” That is an assumption, not an observation.

Question: Were sketching, speaking and observing all offered as meaningful participation choices?

Unknown: What that participant values or intended.

02 / Terminology

Give the right name to the right kind of statement.

A terminology is a set of carefully defined words used consistently. Precise vocabulary prevents an observation being promoted into a diagnosis, a proposed design being mistaken for an outcome, or a possibility becoming an unsupported promise.

Observed

What the scenario directly describes.

Inferred

A possible interpretation.

Proposed

A change we want to try.

Verified

What a relevant check actually supports.

Reveal the difference between an observed event and a verified outcome

Seeing that a participant drew a diagram establishes an action in the fictional account. It does not establish participation quality. A verified outcome would require a defined claim, suitable evidence and an honest scope; even then, the facilitator must respect privacy and context.

03 / Design

Think like a scenario architect.

Before deciding how the activity runs, define the purpose, roles, choices, resources, transitions and uncertainties. Scenario architecture is the arrangement of these parts so they can work together; it is not the invention of outcomes or fictional evidence.

Role A

Observer

Describes what is visible without silently attaching motives. Keeps uncertainty explicit.

Role B

Facilitator

States the purpose, presents choices, manages transitions and protects respectful participation.

Role C

Learning reviewer

Checks whether the design's promises, observations and resulting claims actually match.

Design gate 1: purpose and consent

Can a learner understand the objective, choose how to participate and decline without public pressure?

Design gate 2: environmental fit

Are space, sound, materials, communication methods and transition time suitable for the proposed exercise?

Design gate 3: evidence and boundaries

What would count as an observation? Which conclusions would still remain speculative? Where are the privacy and safeguarding boundaries?

One design challenge

Design two legitimate participation routes for the same paper-bridge activity: a material-building route and a drawing/observation route. Describe one way they can contribute equally to the shared purpose without claiming all participants will prefer them.

See a design that preserves choice

Offer a visible materials area and a sketching/feedback space. Explain that people can move between roles by agreement, contribute verbally or nonverbally, or simply observe. Avoid allocating roles on the basis of an assumed personal limitation; keep the shared purpose clear.

04 / Facilitation

A 20-minute encounter is not a 20-minute lecture.

In this fictional design, the facilitator spends less time explaining and more time supporting informed choice, meaningful activity and a respectful ending. These timings are a planning model, not a confirmed public workshop schedule.

00–03 min

Invitation. Explain the question, materials, available roles and option to observe or decline. State how the encounter will end.

03–06 min

Notice. Introduce the fictional bridge challenge. Ask what can be observed and what remains uncertain.

06–13 min

Create. Allow sketching, making, describing or observing. The facilitator monitors space and invitation conditions—not people's private motives.

13–17 min

Compare. Discuss a rule change. Ask which contribution paths became possible or constrained.

17–20 min

Close. Invite optional reflection, identify one question for revision, and return materials and space to their agreed arrangement.

Facilitator language model

“There are several ways to contribute. You can sketch, work with materials, offer an observation, or simply follow along. We are exploring the design of the activity; no one needs to prove a skill or disclose personal information.”

05 / Evaluation

Evaluate the design before you evaluate people.

Evaluation means examining evidence against an explicit question or criterion. This example asks whether the proposed invitation supports the intended learning purpose. It does not assign performance grades, diagnose participants or claim effectiveness from a fictional run-through.

Design-review questions, not participant scores
Design criterionEvidence to inspectWhat not to claim
Clear purposeInvitation explains the question in plain language.Everyone understands merely because it was stated.
Meaningful choiceAt least two contribution formats are defined.All learners necessarily feel included.
Transition qualityOpening, activity changes and closing are planned.The group will always stay on schedule.
Privacy boundaryNo personal case files, recordings or public disclosure requested.No privacy risk can ever arise.
Evidence integrityObserved facts, hypotheses and proposals use different labels.A good-looking plan proves learning outcomes.
Reviewer question

If a participant chose to watch instead of build, what evidence would justify treating that as informed participation? What information is missing? How could the next invitation be clearer without pressuring the person?

Reveal an appropriately cautious review

The design allows observing as an option, but we cannot infer intention from a person's behaviour. To assess the invitation, review its wording and choices. For real implementation, seek voluntary, appropriately consented feedback without collecting unnecessary personal details.

06 / Improve

Every review should change the next useful question.

Iteration means a new version based on what the previous version taught us. A revision should target a specific issue, keep what already works, and record the difference between observed evidence and a proposed improvement.

Version 01

A spoken invitation with one contribution route.

Review finding

The plan describes only verbal responses; nonverbal contributions are absent from the script.

Version 02 proposal

Add optional sketching and observation, then verify the revised invitation before any real pilot.

Observation-to-design sheet · private worksheet

Use fictional, anonymised or self-created examples. This sheet is for paper or local notes; nothing is submitted here.

  1. Purpose: What question should the activity help people explore?
  2. Observed: Which event can be described without an inferred motive?
  3. Uncertain: What has not been established?
  4. Proposed change: What one condition might be adjusted?
  5. Trade-off: What might become harder or less accessible?
  6. Review criterion: What specific evidence would help us judge the design?
  7. Next version: What would we retain, revise or hold?
See one version-control example

V1 observation: The written invitation described speaking only. V2 proposal: Add an explicit option to sketch or observe. Still unknown: Whether those formats will be meaningful and usable for real learners. Next check: Independent review of the wording and a consent-aware, appropriately supported pilot before making outcome claims.

This educational design studio does not activate real groups, instructor accounts, certificates, clinical intake, data recording or automated scheduling.

Pocket terminology · plain-language glossary

Twelve words a facilitator should not confuse.

Observation
A description of something detectable; not a claim about hidden intentions.
Inference
An interpretation drawn from information that may have more than one explanation.
Hypothesis
A specific possible explanation or prediction that can be investigated.
Context
The surroundings and conditions that change what an action means or makes possible.
Affordance
An opportunity for action arising from a relationship between a person and an environment; it is not a fixed property of an object alone.
Facilitation
Supporting a group process so people can engage with a shared purpose and meaningful choices.
Participation
Involvement in an activity or life situation; not simply how much someone speaks or moves.
Accommodation
An adjustment intended to make participation accessible for a particular need or context.
Criterion
A question or standard chosen in advance for judging a plan or finding.
Evidence
Information relevant to a claim, with a known source and limits.
Feedback loop
A process in which observations about one version inform the next version.
Trade-off
A gain or benefit that may bring a different cost, restriction or uncertainty.

Every term is a working introduction; disciplinary definitions can vary. The point is to make the type and limits of each statement visible.
