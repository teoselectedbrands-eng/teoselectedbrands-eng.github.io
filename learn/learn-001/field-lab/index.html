Observation & Interpretation · Guided field lab · About 15 minutes

One scene. Several stories. What's evidence?

A short, self-paced experiment in seeing, hypothesising and noticing what remains unknown.

No account. No grading. No personal-data submission. Use fictional examples, read in any order, or reveal an example when you want it.

  1. 01 Notice
  2. 02 Interpret
  3. 03 Reframe
  4. 04 Transfer

01 / Notice

Describe what a camera could record.

Picture a fictional corridor. A person approaches a doorway, pauses, turns their head to the right, then walks back the way they came. We can describe the sequence. We cannot see the reason.

From the scene

Observable actions

Approached. Paused. Turned. Walked back.

Notice the verbs: they report actions without claiming a motive or emotion.

Added by a viewer

Possible explanations

Perhaps the room was closed. Perhaps the person remembered another task. Perhaps they noticed a sign.

All could be plausible; none is established by this short description.

Your move

Try writing one observable sentence. Then write a separate sentence beginning with “One possibility is…”. What changed between the sentences?

Reveal a worked example

Observation: The person stopped near the doorway.

Possible explanation: One possibility is that they saw the room was closed.

The second sentence is not wrong to ask about; it becomes misleading only if presented as a fact.

02 / Interpret

What evidence would change your mind?

Interpretations are useful for forming questions, not for declaring hidden intentions. A stronger explanation must identify what would count for and against it.

Hypothesis A

The doorway was inaccessible.

What evidence would help? For example, a visible closed sign or physical obstruction.

Hypothesis B

The person changed plans.

What evidence would help? For example, a later statement from the person about their plan.

Unresolved

We cannot decide yet.

With the available description, uncertainty is a valid and informative conclusion.

Explore why a visible sign would not settle everything

A sign being present could support the idea that the route was unavailable. But we would still not know whether the person saw or read the sign. Evidence has a scope; it cannot answer every question.

03 / Reframe

Move from a person-label to a context-question.

Instant judgment

“They were unmotivated.”

Better observation

“They paused and turned back.”

Better question

“What conditions made the route usable or unusable?”

We are not replacing one confident explanation with another. We are opening questions about information, time, access and surroundings without turning a fictional scene into an individual assessment.

Read the reasoning check

Ask: Which sentence is directly checkable? Which sentence assumes an inner state? What one piece of missing context would most change your interpretation?

04 / Transfer

Use the same method in a different situation.

A student leaves a group table and sits elsewhere. Make a tiny evidence ledger: one visible action, two possible explanations, one unanswered question. Do not use a real person’s private story.

Example of an evidence ledger
ObservationPossible explanationWhat remains unknown?
Moved from one table to another.The first table might have been noisy.Whether noise played any role.
Paused before sitting.They might have been choosing a seat.What they were considering.
Reflect, don't score.

Which question became more interesting after you separated observation from inference? Write one sentence, or keep it as a thought. Nothing on this page stores your answer.
