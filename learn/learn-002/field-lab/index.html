Movement Literacy · Guided field lab · About 15 minutes

Same activity. Different conditions. What changes?

A self-paced task-map experiment about purpose, objects, pathways and participation.

No account. No grading. No personal-data submission. Use fictional examples, read in any order, or reveal an example when you want it.

  1. 01 Map the task
  2. 02 Compare
  3. 03 Redesign
  4. 04 Transfer and connect

01 / Map the task

A movement has a purpose and a place.

Picture a fictional shop counter. A person wants to move a lightweight tote from the counter to a table to make room for another customer. This is an activity with a goal, an object, a route and other people—not merely a command to “lift correctly”.

Goal and object

Clear the counter.

Move the tote to the table. The fictional object has two handles; its exact weight and the individual’s suitability are not given.

Environment and timing

A route around a chair.

The table is several steps away, another person shares the space, and no deadline is specified.

Describe before evaluating

Which details are stated? Which are missing? Try to name a purpose, object, path, time condition and social condition. You can think, sketch or speak your answer; this page does not collect it.

Reveal one possible task map

Purpose: clear the counter. Object: tote. Route: around a chair. Time: unspecified. People: shared shop space. Unknown: exact measurements, object weight, available support and individual preferences.

02 / Compare conditions

Same goal. Different layout.

Imagine two drawings of the same fictional shop. We change the table position, not the purpose. The drawing tells us what happens to the route geometry; it cannot predict how every person will experience the task.

Version A

Table across the room.

The route includes a turn around the chair. The person must navigate a longer path on the drawing.

What to inspect: route length, turning space and where others might pass.

Version B

Table beside the counter.

The drawn route is shorter, but the table may narrow the passage used by other people.

What to inspect: reach, crowding, access and new trade-offs.

Compare claims without turning predictions into facts
QuestionVersion AVersion B
What can we see on the plan?Longer route with a turn.Shorter route near the counter.
What is not yet known?Real-world effort, support, traffic and reach.Whether the tighter passage remains accessible.
What should we avoid claiming?That longer is necessarily harder for everyone.That shorter is automatically better for everyone.

03 / Redesign

One adjustment can create another constraint.

Purpose

Clear the counter while keeping a usable route.

Change

Move the table closer on paper.

Recheck

Did the walking distance shorten? Did the shared passage narrow?

That is a systems question: improving one relationship can change another. Compare the whole arrangement before describing a design as more accessible or efficient. An environment is shared; a solution for one user may affect another user.

Your design experiment

Sketch a third layout: table nearby, but not obstructing the main passage. State two things you would measure or ask about before calling this layout an improvement.

See a possible reasoning pathway

First verify the route and available clearance for all intended users, not just a single actor. Next identify how objects are reached and whether other customers can pass. Measure only what you can verify; do not turn a fictional sketch into an accessibility certification.

04 / Transfer and connect

Take the method to an everyday task.

Imagine a reading room. Books are stored on a high shelf, there is a low table nearby, and a narrow route between chairs. Without assuming anything about an individual, ask: What is the goal? Which object matters? Which movements and conditions matter? What could change?

Movement literacy

What is the activity?

Retrieving a book to read—not merely reaching upward.

Place & Environment

What surrounds it?

Shelf position, chair arrangement, shared route and visibility.

Participation

Who can choose?

Consider different heights, reach preferences and participation options without ranking people.

Read a transfer example

Move frequently chosen books within easier reach and preserve a clear route. This is only a design hypothesis until actual users, constraints and conditions are considered. The conceptual transfer is the questioning method, not proof that skill or outcomes transferred.
