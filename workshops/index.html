PROGRAMME IN PREPARATION

Below the Surface

See what happened before deciding what it means.

A fictional doorway scene gives the group a shared starting point. Someone approaches, pauses, looks right and turns back. Each participant sees the same sequence, but explanations quickly multiply. The workshop makes those differences available for discussion.

What you could leave with

  • Write an observation that another reader can check.
  • Keep an interpretation provisional and identify missing context.
  • Turn a quick judgement into a fairer next question.

Take-home: An evidence-map card with one worked example and a question to revisit.

A sample sequence

  1. Notice the scene individually through words, a drawing or an accessible description.
  2. Compare three viewpoints and map what each makes visible.
  3. Build a visible / inferred / unknown panel in small groups, with a pause between activities.
  4. Use a seated or standing tableau to explore position, then leave with a question map.
Audience, possible format and ways to join

For: Adults, educators and community facilitators

An extended room-based workshop or a facilitated online session. A 150-minute outline is one development option.

Drawing, speaking, listening and observing can be offered alongside movement. An actual delivery plan must confirm access, pauses, materials and supervision with its host.

PROGRAMME IN PREPARATION

Behind the Hill

Make room for another view.

Start with a map that stops at a hill. Each person draws what might be on the other side before comparing answers. The differences become material for a shared investigation rather than a contest to find the most convincing story.

What you could leave with

  • Distinguish an incomplete view from an incorrect claim.
  • Compare perspectives without ranking the people who hold them.
  • Identify what new information could change a shared account.

Take-home: A pocket prompt: what I saw, what I guessed and what else is possible.

A sample sequence

  1. Choose a position on an illustrated map and sketch the view.
  2. Exchange drawings and name the differences.
  3. Use a tabletop screen or room layout to compare lines of sight.
  4. Rebuild the map with evidence, possibilities and unanswered questions clearly marked.
Audience, possible format and ways to join

For: Young adults and adult community groups

A discussion and making workshop; a 90-minute outline is proposed.

PROGRAMME IN PREPARATION

Movement, Space & City

The Movement Map — places we can use.

Two people can follow the same street and notice different things: a shortcut, a place to rest, a difficult crossing or a welcome meeting point. This workshop reads a place through those experiences and asks which details a simple map leaves out.

What you could leave with

  • Map routes, pauses and barriers without using personal addresses.
  • Separate visible features from access assumptions.
  • Develop a question or modest improvement proposal for the people who use the place.

Take-home: A movement-in-context map and a distinction between what is known and what needs checking.

A sample sequence

  1. Sketch a familiar type of place or use the fictional example.
  2. Mark routes, crossings, shade, resting points and boundaries.
  3. Compare perspectives using a tabletop route or a pre-checked optional visit.
  4. Choose one question to investigate and create an anonymous learning panel.
Audience, possible format and ways to join

For: Adults, local groups and people interested in public space

A tabletop mapping workshop or a separately checked outdoor observation. A two-hour outline is a possibility.

Drawing, speaking, listening and observing can be offered alongside movement. An actual delivery plan must confirm access, pauses, materials and supervision with its host.

PROGRAMME IN PREPARATION

Soft Social

Make something together.

Conversation is one way to meet; making something can be another. At a shared table, people choose a shape, word or movement mark and gradually build a mural or modular arrangement. A contribution can be quiet, spoken or made alongside someone else.

What you could leave with

  • Notice how materials and setup offer different ways to join.
  • Share decisions about a collective object.
  • Describe a participation choice without assigning a motive to someone else.

Take-home: A small record of one making choice and an invitation the group found useful.

A sample sequence

  1. Begin at an open making station without a compulsory introduction.
  2. Choose materials and add a first contribution.
  3. Arrange, exchange or modify pieces in pairs, independently or silently.
  4. Look at the whole work and optionally discuss how it changed.
Audience, possible format and ways to join

For: Adults who prefer low-pressure participation

A small creative gathering; a 75-minute outline is proposed.

PROGRAMME IN PREPARATION

Changing the Rules

A cooperative play lab.

Keep the same people and objects, then change one rule. The task may become quicker, more collaborative or easier for a different person to join. This lab uses the comparison itself as a learning tool.

What you could leave with

  • Identify how a rule changes roles, choices and interaction.
  • Design an alternative with a clear purpose.
  • Describe participation without treating a game as a test of a person.

Take-home: A rule card describing the original version, one change and what to notice next.

A sample sequence

  1. Understand a simple shared game before trying a version.
  2. Record who can contribute, what the task requires and what remains unclear.
  3. Change one rule and offer a seated or lower-intensity way to join.
  4. Compare versions and make an adaptation card.
Audience, possible format and ways to join

For: Adult learners and educators; school adaptations require a separate plan

A room-based group workshop with tabletop and seated alternatives; a 90-minute outline is proposed.

PROGRAMME IN PREPARATION

A Classroom That Moves

An educator lab for purposeful activity.

Choose a transition in a fictional school day: arriving, regrouping after a break or preparing for a shared task. Educators work from the actual constraints of a classroom to design an activity with a clear purpose and more than one way to participate.

What you could leave with

  • Connect a brief activity with a learning or transition purpose.
  • Adapt space, noise, instructions and participation choices.
  • Produce a plan another educator can understand and review.

Take-home: A teacher-facing activity plan, ready for the school’s own review before a pilot.

A sample sequence

  1. Select a classroom transition and draw the available space.
  2. Compare two short activity prototypes.
  3. Design one activity with a quieter or seated alternative.
  4. Teach the plan to another educator and revise it using practical feedback.
Audience, possible format and ways to join

For: Teachers, school coordinators and adult education teams

A school-team or online planning workshop; a 90-minute outline is proposed.

The Movement Reading Room

A retained Aetherion concept for adults interested in sport and movement culture. Bring a permitted video sequence or use a fictional scene, describe what happens, compare readings, then consider how the goal and setting change the action.

A sample encounter moves through noticing, comparison, context and a better question. The take-home object is a short field note, with optional spoken or drawn participation.
